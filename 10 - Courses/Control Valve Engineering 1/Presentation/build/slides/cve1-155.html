Working the Steam Case — Design ED Valve

4-inch ANSI Class 300 Design ED valve, linear cage, installed with reducers in a 6-inch line, superheated steam.
  1. Given: w = 125,000 lb/h, P1 = 500 psig (514.7 psia), P2 = 250 psig (264.7 psia), x = ΔP/P1 = 0.49, T1 = 500°F, g1 = 1.0434 lb/ft³, k = 1.28
  2. N6 = 63.3 (mass-flow units, g1 specified)
  3. Between the 4-in valve and its 6-in reducers: ΣK = 1.5·(1−(4/6)²)² = 0.463 → Fp = 0.95, the same method m1 teaches
  4. With Y = 0.73 (derived on the next two slides): Cv = w ÷ (N6·Fp·Y·√(x·P1·g1)) = 125,000 ÷ (63.3·0.95·0.73·√(0.49·514.7·1.0434)) = 176
Common failureTreating Y as 1.0, as if this were still a liquid calculation. Dropping Y out of the denominator here returns Cv ≈ 128 — about 27% smaller than the true 176 — because the expansion factor is doing real work in this equation, not sitting there as a formality. A calculation that quietly skips Y specs a valve that reads as adequate on paper and can’t deliver rated flow in the field.
Predict it before the next two slides derive it: with x = ΔP/P1 = 0.49 — a large pressure ratio, not a small one — would you expect the expansion factor Y to land close to 1.0 (barely compressible) or well below it? See how close your instinct comes to the derived Y = 0.73.
